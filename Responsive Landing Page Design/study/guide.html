Your whole project as a story

No jargon. Read this first, and every other unit will make more sense.

Chapter 1 · The money

Imagine you've got £5,000. You put half into Shell and half into BP, two giant oil companies listed in London. That pair of shares is your basket.

Every day the basket's value moves a little. Some days up, some days down. Your project is about guessing those moves before they happen.

Chapter 2 · The hunch

Shell and BP make most of their money from oil. So when the price of oil jumps (say, because of a war or a big announcement), their shares often react.

Your hunch: if I know what oil and the shares did recently, can I make a better guess about what the shares do next?

Chapter 3 · Two questions

You're asking the computer two things about each day:

  1. "Will my basket go up tomorrow?" Yes or no.
  2. "How bumpy will the next week be?" Calm, or a rollercoaster?

The second one sounds harder, but it's actually easier: bumpy weeks tend to come in clusters, like bad weather. Whether it goes up tomorrow is close to a coin flip.

Chapter 4 · How the computer learns

You have about 10 years of history, roughly 2,700 trading days. For each old day you know two things: what the clues looked like (recent moves, how busy trading was, what oil did) and what actually happened next.

You show the computer thousands of these "clues → what happened" examples. It looks for a rule that links them. That's all "machine learning" means here: finding a pattern in examples where you already know the answer.

Chapter 5 · Simple vs clever

You try two kinds of rule-finder for each question: a simple one (a straight-line rule, easy to explain) and a clever one (a "random forest": hundreds of mini decision-flowcharts that vote).

You also try each one with oil data and without it. If adding oil makes the guesses better, that answers your hunch.

Chapter 6 · The fair test

The computer learns from 2015–2021, you fine-tune using 2022–2023, and the final exam is 2024–2025, years it has never seen. Just like real life, where you can only learn from the past.

The big danger is accidental cheating: letting a tiny bit of "tomorrow" sneak into today's clues. For example, using an oil price that was only published after you made your guess. It makes your results look amazing, but they'd be fake. Most of your plan is about preventing this.

Chapter 7 · The lazy guess

To know if your model is any good, you compare it to a lazy guess: "it'll go up, because it usually does" or "next week will be as bumpy as last week". If your clever model can't beat the lazy guess, it hasn't learned anything useful.

Chapter 8 · The surprises

Markets have sudden shocks nobody saw coming: Covid in 2020, the Ukraine war in 2022. These are called black swans. Your models can't predict them, because they learn from the past and these were new.

That's fine. You just need to say so honestly, look at how your models behaved during those crazy periods, and not claim more than your results show.

Chapter 9 · What Assignment 1 actually is

You're not doing any of this yet. Assignment 1 is just the plan: which data, which question, how you'll test it, and what could go wrong. About 500 words. Your draft already says all of this; the rest of this guide helps you understand every word of it.

What data science is

Using recorded information to work out something about the world: either to predict what you haven't seen, or to explain how things work.

The textbook's definition: data science is interpreting information to draw inferences or make predictions about the world. It sits where three things overlap:

  • Computational expertise: coding, algorithms, software. You have this.
  • Quantitative reasoning: statistics, models, maths. This guide builds it.
  • Domain knowledge: deep understanding of a field. For you: markets and trading technology.

The lecturer quotes a colleague: "there's no such thing as a data scientist", meaning you are always a data scientist in something. He is one in infectious-disease genomics. You are becoming one in finance.

Your domain knowledge is what lets you spot that Brent is published at a different time from the London close, or that Shell changed its share structure in 2022. No algorithm would notice either.

When does information become data?

Information is everywhere. It becomes data when someone deliberately records it in a way that lets many records be compared.

The textbook's definition: data are information that is recorded, informative and comparable. A data set is usually many data points that are reproducible, individually dispensable and exchangeable with one another.

The Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa (Babylon, observations from about 3,600 years ago) records the first and last days Venus was visible each year for 21 years. Several dates are physically impossible, probably copying errors, yet the data set survives because no single row is indispensable. The much older Warren Field calendar in Scotland (about 10,000 years old) tracked lunar months with twelve pits.

Purpose matters: one cave painting is a record; the same painting becomes a data point when it is one of many being compared in a survey.

The central idea: the data generating process

Data doesn't appear by magic. Something happened in the world, then someone recorded it in a particular way. That whole chain is the data generating process, and it's the single most important idea in the course.

Split it into two parts:

  • The scientific process: what is really going on in the world, governed by hidden quantities called parameters (the effect of a drug, the "true value" of a stock as opposed to its price).
  • The sampling process: how it came to be recorded. This can add distortions and biases.
World (hidden parameters) → sampling process → the data in your file

Prediction can often skip the details of the world, which is why flexible "black box" models work. Inference runs the chain in reverse, from data back to parameters. But neither can skip the sampling process: if you don't know the provenance of your data, you can't trust what you learn from it. So the textbook's verdict: a fully black-box approach to data science is never justified.

For you. Scientific process: news, supply and demand, and investor decisions move oil and share prices. Sampling process: exchange matching engines record trades; price-reporting agencies assess Brent; LSEG adjusts and publishes; you export. Each step can distort (adjustments, timestamps, which firms you chose).

Supervised vs unsupervised

Supervised means you learn with the answers at the back of the book. Unsupervised means you get no answers and just look for patterns. You have the answers (you know what the shares did next), so yours is supervised.

Supervised: you have examples with the right answer attached. "Here were Monday's clues; Tuesday's return was positive." The model learns to map clues to answers.

Unsupervised: no answer column. The model looks for structure on its own, e.g. grouping stocks that move together.

Yours is supervised, because for every historical day you know what happened next.

Classification vs regression

Classification answers a yes/no or pick-a-box question ("will it go up?"). Regression answers a how-much question ("how much will it wobble?"). You're doing one of each.

TypePredictsYour target
ClassificationA categoryBasket up tomorrow? 1 = yes, 0 = no
RegressionA numberStd dev of basket returns over next 5 days

You have two targets, so you're doing one of each. Two targets × two models each = four models, each run with and without Brent.

X and y: clues and answers

X is everything you show the computer (the clues). y is the thing you want it to guess (the answer). Each day is one row: clues on the left, answer on the right.

Features (X) are the clues the model sees. Target (y) is the answer it's trying to predict.

One day becomes a vector, an ordered list of numbers:

[0.005, 0.012, 0.018, 1.3, -0.02]

= yesterday's return, 5-day return, volatility, relative volume, Brent return. The order defines the meaning.

Stack ~2,700 trading days of these and you get a matrix (a table). That's X. Next to it sits a column y.

The mean

Add everything up and divide by how many there are. The "typical" value.

r‾=1n∑i=1nri

Five daily basket returns: +1%, −2%, +3%, 0%, +2.5%.

Sum = 1 − 2 + 3 + 0 + 2.5 = 4.5. Divide by n = 5: mean = 0.9%.

r = [1, -2, 3, 0, 2.5]
mean = sum(r) / len(r)        # 0.9

For daily stock returns the mean is tiny (around 0.03% a day), much smaller than the typical move. That's why direction is so hard to predict: the "signal" is small next to the noise.

The normal distribution (bell curve)

The classic hill-shaped curve: most values near the middle, fewer and fewer further out. Heights of people roughly follow it. Stock returns only roughly do.

f(x)=1σ2πexp(−(x−μ)22σ2)

Don't memorise it. Read it as: "the further x is from μ, measured in units of σ, the faster the height drops". The front fraction just makes the total area equal 1 (probabilities must add to 100%).

Probability basics

Probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). 0.5 = coin flip.

P(notA)=1−P(A)P(AandB)=P(A)P(B)ifindependent

If each day is up with probability 0.52 and days are independent, the chance of 3 up days in a row = 0.52 × 0.52 × 0.52 ≈ 0.14.

Independent means knowing one tells you nothing about the other. Stock directions are close to independent day to day. Stock volatility is not (bumpy days cluster), which is why your volatility target is more predictable.

Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

"The chance of A, given that B happened." The vertical bar ∣ reads as "given". And: the chance of A given B is not the same as the chance of B given A.

P(A∣B)=P(B∣A)P(A)P(B)

Lateral flow test from Lecture 4.6, early pandemic: 2% infected. Test catches 92.5% of infections and wrongly flags 2.1% of healthy people.

P(positive) = 0.925 × 0.02 + 0.021 × 0.98 = 0.0185 + 0.0206 = 0.0391.

P(infected | positive) = 0.0185 / 0.0391 ≈ 47%. A positive test was more likely wrong than right!

That's the base rate fallacy, and it's the maths behind PPV in the Evaluation unit. The rarer the thing, the more false alarms swamp the true hits.

Expected value: the long-run average

If you repeated something forever, what would you get on average? Multiply each outcome by its chance and add up.

E[X]=∑xxP(X=x)

A strategy is right 55% of the time and makes +1% when right, but loses −1.5% when wrong.

E = 0.55 × 1 + 0.45 × (−1.5) = 0.55 − 0.675 = −0.125% per trade. Right more often than wrong, and still losing money.

This is the maths behind "accuracy ≠ profitability" in your plan.

Covariance and correlation

Do two things move together? Correlation is a score from −1 (always opposite) through 0 (no link) to +1 (always together).

Corr(x,y)=∑i(xi−x‾)(yi−y‾)∑i(xi−x‾)2∑i(yi−y‾)2

How to read it: the top multiplies "how far x is from its average" by "how far y is from its average", day by day. If both are above average on the same days (or both below), the products are positive and pile up. The bottom just rescales the answer to sit between −1 and +1.

Shell and BP daily returns are strongly positively correlated (both are oil majors). Brent and the shares are positively correlated on the same day. Neither fact says anything about predicting tomorrow.

Logs and exponentials

ex (exp) means "grow continuously". ln (natural log) is its undo button. Their superpower: logs turn multiplication into addition.

ln(ex)=xln(a⋅b)=lna+lnb

Prices compound by multiplying (×1.02, then ×0.99…). Taking logs turns that chain into a simple sum, which is easier for maths and models. That's why finance people like log returns. Logs also appear in logistic regression and in the "log loss" used to train classifiers.

Vectors, dot products and matrices

A vector is a list of numbers. A dot product multiplies two lists item by item and adds the results. Almost every model you'll meet, including neural networks, is built on this one operation.

w⋅x=∑j=1pwjxj=w1x1+w2x2+⋯+wpxp

Weights w = [2, −1, 0.5], features x = [0.01, 0.02, 1.3]. w·x = 0.02 − 0.02 + 0.65 = 0.65.

Stack every day's feature vector as a row and you get the matrix X (shape n × p: n days, p features). Then Xw computes the dot product for every day in one go: one prediction per row.

import numpy as np
w = np.array([2, -1, 0.5]); x = np.array([0.01, 0.02, 1.3])
w @ x              # 0.65, the dot product
X @ w              # X has shape (n_days, 3) → n_days predictions

Slopes, derivatives and finding the bottom

A derivative is the slope: if I nudge this input a tiny bit, how much does the output change? At the bottom of a valley the slope is zero. "Training a model" means finding the bottom of an error valley.

ddxx2=2x

For x2: at x = 3 the slope is 6 (steep, going up), at x = 0 the slope is 0 (the bottom). With many inputs, the list of slopes is called the gradient, written ∇. It points uphill, so walking the opposite way takes you down. That's gradient descent (Models unit).

Why we record things at all

Knowledge that lives only in people's heads dies with them. Writing it down lets each generation build on the last. Digitising it makes it searchable in seconds.

Tacit knowledge (know-how, intuition, experience) is hard to record and lasts one lifetime. Recorded knowledge accumulates: the textbook calls it "the basis of civilisation".

The week's running example: after the Second World War the Allies tried to identify and reward civilians who had helped people escape occupied France. The networks had run by word of mouth, because anything on paper could get someone killed. Reconstructing who did what took a huge campaign of witness statements, filed on paper and found through card indexes. About 22,000 helpers were identified.

Bits, bytes and binary

Computers store everything as 0s and 1s. One 0-or-1 is a bit. Eight bits make a byte, which can hold a whole number from 0 to 255.

Binary is base two: each place is worth twice the one to its right (… 8, 4, 2, 1), just as decimal places are worth ten times more.

10 in binary: 8 + 2 → 1010. 13: 8 + 4 + 1 → 1101. 6: 4 + 2 → 110.

Text files, binary files and UTF-8

There are two kinds of file. Text files are meant to be read by people. Binary files (images, audio, programs) aren't. Underneath, both are just bytes; text files follow a convention for turning bytes into letters.

That convention is a character encoding. The dominant one is UTF-8, which uses 1 to 4 bytes per character:

Sound as numbers: sampling

A sound is a wave. To store it, measure the wave's height thousands of times a second and write down each measurement. That's sampling.

A WAV file records the amplitude at fixed time steps, typically 44,100 times a second (one sample every ~23 microseconds), each as a 16-bit whole number. A header describes the format. Three numbers matter:

  • Sampling rate sets the highest frequency you can capture. The Nyquist theorem: sample at least twice the highest frequency you care about. Humans hear up to about 20,000 Hz, so 44,100 Hz is enough.
  • Bit depth sets the dynamic range (how fine the loudness steps are).
  • Bitrate is data per second, the storage cost.
bitrate=samplingrate×bitdepth×channels

CD-quality stereo: 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bits per second ≈ 176,400 bytes per second, so one minute ≈ 10.6 MB.

Sampling is everywhere in market data. A daily close is one sample per day of a price that moves continuously. Sample too slowly and you miss what happened in between: your daily data cannot see intraday swings, which is one reason 5 daily returns give a noisy volatility estimate.

Images as numbers, and compression

A picture is a grid of tiny dots (pixels). Each dot is three numbers from 0 to 255: how much red, green and blue.

Red is (255, 0, 0); white (255, 255, 255); black (0, 0, 0); yellow (255, 255, 0). The grid is flattened row by row, like reading a page, after a header giving width, height and bit depth.

A full-HD image is 1,920 × 1,080 = 2,073,600 pixels × 3 bytes ≈ 6.2 MB uncompressed. Hence compression.

PNG's trick: store the differences between neighbouring pixels. In smooth regions these are tiny numbers that compress well, and adding them back up recovers the original exactly.

You already use the same trick. A return is the difference between neighbouring prices. Storing changes instead of levels is why returns are small, comparable numbers.

Metadata: the half of the story the file can't tell

A CSV full of numbers doesn't say what the columns mean, what units they're in, where they came from or whether you're allowed to use them. That information is metadata: data about data.

The textbook shows a tidy CSV row and asks: what scale is the award grade on? Does "-" mean none, refused or unknown? When was it recorded? On what legal basis may we use it? None of it can be read from the data. Metadata records:

  • the schema: each field's type, valid values and missing-value codes;
  • provenance: source, date, who compiled it;
  • licence and, for personal data, the lawful basis under GDPR.

GDPR says personal data must be processed lawfully for a specific purpose, limited to what's needed, accurate, kept no longer than necessary and kept securely. There are six lawful bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests (the one used in the lecture). A named data controller is accountable.

FAIR principles: data should be Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable. A YAML sidecar (a small key: value text file kept beside the data) is one way to write metadata down.

In finance, metadata is called reference data and it is a whole industry: instrument identifiers, currencies, trading calendars, corporate actions. Most real-world data bugs in trading systems are reference-data bugs, like pence quoted as pounds.

Relational databases: keys instead of repetition

One giant table repeats the same details over and over. A relational database splits the data into several small tables, each about one thing, linked by ID columns called keys.

Replication: are your days "reruns" of each other?

To learn from the past you must assume past days are fair copies of future days, apart from the features you've measured. If the market's behaviour changes, that assumption breaks.

Simpson's paradox

A pattern can flip direction depending on whether you look at all the data together or split it into groups. It's a symptom that a hidden third variable is involved.

  • Berkeley 1973: men had a higher admission rate overall, yet women did as well or better within most departments. Women applied more to departments that rejected most applicants (a mediator: choice of subject).
  • The weekend effect: weekend hospital admissions had higher death rates. But sicker patients come in at weekends; blood tests explained much of the gap (a confounder: severity).
  • Elite performers: studying only people who excelled as youths or adults made youth success look harmful (a collider: selection).

How it could bite you: pooling 2015–2025, oil and basket returns might look strongly linked because a few crisis periods dominate. Within calm periods the link could be weak, or even different in sign. Split by period before you trust a pooled pattern.

In finance: the same traps, with money on them

Every pitfall in this unit has a well-known finance version. Knowing them is a large part of being trusted with data in this industry.

  • Confounding → "it's just beta". A strategy that makes money might simply be riding the whole market up. Finance controls for this confounder with factor models: regress returns on the market (and other factors) and see what's left.
  • Survivorship bias → fund league tables. Databases that drop dead funds and delisted stocks make average performance look better than it was. Backtests on "today's index members" have the same flaw.

  • Simpson's paradox → performance attribution. A fund can beat its benchmark in every sector and still lag overall if it held more of the weak sectors.
  • Randomisation → A/B tests on execution. Some asset managers randomly route orders between brokers' algorithms ("algo wheels") to compare them fairly. Why the volatility target is more predictable

AI, machine learning and data science

AI is machines doing tasks that would normally need a human mind. Machine learning is the main way that's done today: algorithms that find patterns in examples. Data science is the wider craft of asking good questions and checking the answers.

The textbook's definitions:

  • Artificial intelligence: the ability of machines to perform tasks that would otherwise require human cognition: perception, reasoning, learning, language, decision-making. It's a convenient definition because it sidesteps what "intelligence" really is.
  • Machine learning: a branch of AI in which general-purpose algorithms exploit patterns in data to predict or decide. Most of what people call AI is machine learning under the hood.
  • Data science: the wider discipline of asking the right questions, collecting and cleaning data, and interpreting results critically. Machine learning supplies its analytical engine.

Rules-based AI vs machine learning

Old-style AI: a programmer writes the rules. Machine learning: you give the machine examples with the right answers and it works out the rules itself.

Rules-based: rules (written by hand) + data → answers
Machine learning: data + answers → rules (the trained model)

Rules-based AI. In the 1970s Stanford's MYCIN diagnosed bacterial infections with around 600 hand-written rules from specialists. It worked well, but only in its narrow domain and only while the rules held. Rules-based systems are brittle but explainable and auditable, which machine learning models often aren't.

The other strands: robotics (systems that sense and act, with real-time, safety-critical control) and agentic AI (systems that plan, use tools, run code and work towards goals with little human input).

Banks still run a lot of rules-based logic, because rules can be audited: pre-trade risk limits ("reject any order over £X"), sanctions screening and many compliance checks are explicit rules. Machine learning is layered on where patterns are too complex to write down, such as fraud detection.

Why it took off when it did

Three things arrived together: mountains of data, cheap fast computing, and better algorithms.

  1. Data. Every search, health record, satellite image and genome is a potential training example.
  2. Computing power. Graphics chips (GPUs) built for video games turned out to be ideal for the matrix arithmetic inside machine learning. Cloud computing made it affordable.
  3. Algorithms. Neural networks learned complex patterns directly from raw data. Then the transformer architecture unlocked language.

The first multipurpose programmable computer with electronic memory, the Manchester "Baby", was built in 1948. The AI revolution "did not happen overnight": decades of quiet progress crossed a threshold.

Models and parameters: the tuning dials

A model is a formula with adjustable dials. Training turns the dials until the formula's predictions match the examples as closely as possible.

In the textbook's words, a model is a mathematical function that predicts data, and its tuneable settings are parameters. Updating them is how the model learns.

Because machine learning usually targets prediction, the models needn't be interpretable. The best performers are often the hardest to read: black boxes.

Don't assume trained parameters mean something in the real world. They're tuning dials, not facts about markets. Linear models are the happy exception: their parameters can be read as slopes.

The loss and the training loop

The loss is a single number for "how wrong is the model right now?" Training is a loop: predict, measure the loss, nudge the dials to make it smaller, repeat.

  • In classification, a simple loss counts misclassifications.
  • In regression, a simple loss adds up the (usually squared) distances between predictions and true values.
features → model (parameters θ) → prediction ŷ → compare with truth y → loss → nudge θ → repeat

Training is iterative. If you chart the loss, it typically collapses within a few steps and then levels off; a stopping rule ends training when improvement becomes negligible.

The loss often carries a second term, the regulariser, which pushes parameters towards zero so the model doesn't fit noise.

Too simple, about right, too flexible

A model that's too simple misses the pattern. One that's too flexible memorises the training examples, noise and all, and falls apart on new data.

Linear regression

Draw the best straight-line rule through the data: "start at this number, add a bit for each clue". Simple and easy to explain.

Predicts a number as a starting level plus weighted contributions from each feature:

ŷ = b₀ + b₁·vol_5 + b₂·vol_20 + b₃·brent_ret + …

Training finds the coefficients (b's) that minimise squared error on the training data. Easy to read: b₁ tells you how much predicted volatility rises per unit of recent volatility.

A straight line can predict negative volatility, which is impossible. Your plan sets those to zero, decided in advance. That's the right move (np.clip(pred, 0, None)).

Logistic regression

Same idea as linear regression, but the answer is squeezed into a percentage chance between 0% and 100%, like "56% chance it goes up".

Despite the name, it's for classification. It builds a linear score z, then squashes it into a probability between 0 and 1 with the sigmoid:

p = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))
Drag the score

With a 50% threshold, 56% → predict "up". The 56% is an estimate, not a guarantee.

Decision tree

A game of 20 Questions: "Was last month bumpy? Was today busy?" Each answer leads down a branch to a guess.

A flowchart of yes/no questions learned from data:

vol_20 > 1.8%? ├─ yes → relvol > 1.5? ─ yes → predict 2.4% vol │ └ no → predict 1.9% └─ no → predict 1.1%

The tree picks the questions and thresholds that best split the training data. Deep trees memorise noise, so you cap depth.

Random forest

Ask hundreds of slightly different decision trees and go with the group's answer. A crowd usually beats one person.

Hundreds of trees, each trained on a random resample of rows (bootstrap) and allowed to consider a random subset of features at each split. Classification: trees vote. Regression: trees average.

Asking 300 slightly different analysts and averaging their answers. Individual quirks cancel out.

Strength: captures interactions (e.g. "high volume matters only when volatility is already high") that a linear model misses. Weakness: can still overfit; harder to interpret.

Parameters, hyperparameters, regularisation

Parameters are what the computer learns. Hyperparameters are the settings you choose. Regularisation is a rule that stops the model getting too complicated.

  • Parameter: learned from data (a regression coefficient, a tree's split threshold).
  • Hyperparameter: a setting you choose (max tree depth, number of trees, regularisation strength C).
  • Regularisation: a penalty that discourages complexity, e.g. shrinking large coefficients. In trees: max_depth, min_samples_leaf.

Choose hyperparameters using the validation set, never the test set.

"Limited tuning" and "restrained model complexity" are regularisation choices.

Scaling (standardisation)

Putting all clues on the same scale so a big-number clue doesn't drown out a small-number one. Like converting everything to the same currency before comparing.

Features live on different scales (returns ~0.01, relative volume ~1.3). Standardisation rescales each one to (value − training mean) / training std dev.

It matters for logistic/linear regression with regularisation (otherwise the penalty treats features unfairly). Trees don't care: they only ask "above or below a threshold?".

Calculating the mean and std on the whole dataset leaks future information into training. Fit the scaler on training data only.

Overfitting

Memorising the exam answers instead of learning the subject. Great on questions you've seen, bad on new ones.

The model memorises quirks of the training data instead of general patterns. Signature: great training score, poor score on new dates.

With noisy financial data and ~1,750 training rows, overfitting is the default danger. That's why you compare a flexible model against a simple one and both against a baseline.

Linear regression: loss and solution

Linear regression's score is "average squared miss". There's a formula that jumps straight to the best line, no trial and error needed.

y^i=β0+β1xi1+⋯+βpxip=β0+β⋅xi

You don't need to derive the last one. Read it as "a matrix calculation that finds the β making MSE as small as possible". X⊤ is X flipped on its side (transpose); (…)−1 is the matrix version of "divide by".

Suppose training finds: predicted vol = 0.3% + 0.55 × vol_20 + 0.15 × vol_5.

If vol_20 = 1.6% and vol_5 = 2.0%: ŷ = 0.3 + 0.88 + 0.30 = 1.48%. Each coefficient says how much the prediction moves per unit of that feature.

Because errors are squared, one 5-point miss counts as much as twenty-five 1-point misses. A few crisis days can drag the whole line. Keep that in mind for Black Swans.

Logistic regression in depth

It's linear regression whose output is pushed through an S-curve to become a probability, and it's scored by how surprised it was by what actually happened.

z=β0+β⋅xp=σ(z)=11+e−z
lnp1−p=z

Odds are p/(1−p): 60% = odds of 1.5 to 1. The model is linear in the log of the odds. So a coefficient β means "a one-unit rise in this feature multiplies the odds by eβ". With β = 0.2 on a standardised feature, one standard deviation more multiplies the odds of an up day by 1.22.

L=−1n∑i=1n[yilnpi+(1−yi)ln(1−pi)]

Actual: down day (y = 0).

  • Model said p = 0.60 → loss = −ln(0.40) = 0.92
  • Model said p = 0.99 → loss = −ln(0.01) = 4.61

Being confidently wrong is punished hard. That pushes the model to give honest, cautious probabilities.

Regularisation as an equation

Add a "complexity tax" to the score. The model now has to balance fitting the data with keeping its coefficients small.

Lridge=MSE+λ∑j=1pβj2

λ (lambda) sets the tax rate. λ = 0: no tax. Huge λ: every β squashed to ~0 (the model predicts the average). scikit-learn's LogisticRegression uses C = 1/λ: smaller C = stronger regularisation. A common confusion.

The tax only makes sense if features share a scale, which is why you standardise first:

x'ij=xij−μjtrainσjtrain

How a tree picks its questions

At each step the tree tries every feature and every cut-off, and keeps the question that best separates the data into "purer" groups.

G=1−∑kpk2

pk is the share of class k in a group. A 50/50 mix: G = 1 − (0.25 + 0.25) = 0.5 (messiest). All one class: G = 0 (pure). 90/10: G = 0.18.

100 days: 52 up, 48 down → G = 0.499.

Question "vol_20 > 1.8%?" splits them into 40 days (30 up, 10 down, G = 0.375) and 60 days (22 up, 38 down, G = 0.464).

Weighted: 0.4 × 0.375 + 0.6 × 0.464 = 0.429. Improvement 0.07. The tree keeps whichever question gives the biggest improvement.

For regression trees (your volatility target), the same idea uses variance instead of Gini, and each leaf predicts the average of its training days.

Why a forest beats a tree

Averaging many noisy guesses cancels out much of the noise, as long as the guesses aren't all making the same mistakes.

Var(f‾)=ρσ2+1−ρBσ2
B

More trees shrink the second term towards zero. But the first term stays, unless the trees are made different (low ρ). That's why random forests give each tree a random sample of rows (bootstrap) and a random subset of features at each split.

The bias–variance trade-off

Dartboard: bias = consistently aiming at the wrong spot (model too simple). Variance = darts scattered everywhere (model too sensitive to the training data). Noise = the board itself wobbles, and nothing fixes that.

E[(y−f^(x))2]=Bias2+Variance+σnoise2

Simple models: high bias, low variance. Flexible models: low bias, high variance. In daily returns the noise term is enormous, so a flexible model mostly ends up fitting noise. That's the deep reason your plan favours "restrained model complexity", and why comparing a simple model against a flexible one is a good experiment.

Leakage vs overfitting

Overfitting is memorising. Leakage is cheating: accidentally letting the model see the answer. Leakage is worse because your score looks great but is fake.

Overfitting = learning noise. Leakage = using information you wouldn't have had at prediction time. Leakage is worse because it gives you a falsely great score.

Leakage examples in your project:

  • Scaler fitted on all 2015–2025 data.

Mean absolute error (MAE)

MAE vs RMSE

Both measure "how far off on average". RMSE squares the misses first, so a single big miss hurts it far more.

MAE=1n∑i=1n|yi−y^i|

Four volatility errors: 0.1, 0.1, 0.1, 2.0 (one crisis week).

Your plan uses MAE: easy to explain and less dominated by black-swan weeks. Reporting RMSE too would show how badly models handle extreme weeks.

Is 53% really better than 50%?

With a few hundred test days, luck alone moves accuracy by a couple of percent. You need to know how big that luck is before you celebrate.

SE=p(1−p)n

A coin-flip model (p = 0.5) on n = 500 test days: SE = √(0.25 / 500) ≈ 0.022 = 2.2 percentage points.

53% is only (53 − 50) / 2.2 ≈ 1.3 standard errors above chance. Pure luck produces that fairly often (about 1 time in 10). Not convincing.

To make 53% sit 2 standard errors above 50%, you'd need roughly 1,100 independent test days.

So in your write-up, say "53% vs 50% baseline is within what chance could produce" rather than "the model works".

Overlapping windows shrink your evidence

Monday's 5-day window and Tuesday's share four of the same days. They're nearly the same measurement taken twice, not two independent tests.

Rough rule: with 5-day overlapping targets, 500 test rows carry about as much information as ~100 independent ones. So uncertainty is larger than a naive calculation suggests (a bit more than twice as large, since √5 ≈ 2.2).

Practical response: report differences between models cautiously, and compare models on the same days (paired), e.g. "model A beat the baseline in 58% of weeks".

The multiple testing trap

Try enough things and one will look good by luck. Like flipping 20 coins and celebrating the one that landed heads 5 times in a row.

P(atleastonefalsewin)=1−(1−α)m

Each test has a 5% fluke rate (α = 0.05). Try m = 20 feature sets: 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64% chance at least one "works" by pure luck.

That's why you choose on validation, touch test once, and keep tuning "limited", as your plan says.

What inference is for

Prediction asks "what will happen?". Inference asks "what is the world like, and how does it work?". You can't look the answer up, so you reason backwards from data to a hidden number.

That hidden number is a parameter: a quantity that governs the data generating process, such as how much better one drug is than another. Lecture 9.2 gives inference four aims:

  1. Build a probability model connecting the seen (data) to the unseen (parameters). This turns a real-world question into a mathematical one.
  2. Estimate the parameters: your best guess, the point estimate.
  3. Quantify uncertainty: a range of plausible values, the interval estimate (confidence or credibility interval).
  4. Test hypotheses: make a yes/no judgement about the world, with a level of uncertainty attached.
The lecture's examples: do two HIV treatments differ in effectiveness? What else affects how fast HIV progresses? Does genetics influence years in education? Does education translate into income?

Probability models and the likelihood

An inference model doesn't just predict the data; it says how probable any data would be. Feed in the data you actually saw and you get the likelihood.

Inference models are stochastic (they include randomness), so they describe the noise as well as the signal. That puts probability theory at the centre of inference.

L(θ)=P(data∣θ)

The likelihood is the probability of the observed data given the parameters. Both main inference methods, maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference, are built on it. In practice people work with its logarithm, the log-likelihood, because logs turn products into sums.

Maximum likelihood: the lecture's HIV example

Try every possible value of the parameter and keep the one that makes your actual data most probable. That value is the maximum likelihood estimate.

A 1990s randomised trial (ACTG 175) compared HIV treatment with zidovudine alone against zidovudine in combination with another drug. HIV destroys white blood cells called CD4 T cells, so the outcome was the change in log₁₀ CD4 count after 20 weeks. The question "do the treatments differ?" becomes a parameter: β, the difference in mean outcome between the two groups, in a model written outcome ~ treatment.

The log-likelihood peaks at β̂ = 0.094: the maximum likelihood estimate.

It's on a log₁₀ scale, so undo the log: 10^0.094 = 1.24. CD4 counts were about 24% higher on combination therapy after 20 weeks.

This style of reasoning, "which explanation makes what I saw most likely?", is called abductive logic.

Interval estimates: how sure are you?

A single best guess hides how uncertain you are. An interval gives the range of parameter values that fit the data nearly as well as the best one.

In maximum likelihood, the interval comes straight from the log-likelihood curve:

{θ:ℓ(θ^)−ℓ(θ)≤1.92}

Draw a line 1.92 units below the peak of the log-likelihood; every θ above that line is in the 95% confidence interval. (1.92 is half of 3.84, a standard cut-off from statistical theory.)

The formal meaning is awkward. Imagine repeating the whole data generating process many times and building an interval each time: the true parameter would fall inside 95% of those intervals. It is not "a 95% chance the truth is in this particular interval".

Why more data helps: LLN and CLT

Average enough independent random things and (1) the average settles down to the true value, and (2) its wobble around the truth looks like a bell curve, shrinking as 1/√n.

  • Law of large numbers: p^ gets closer to p as n grows.
  • Central limit theorem: for large n, the estimate is roughly normal around the truth:
p^∼N(p,p(1−p)n)(approximately)SE(p^)=p^(1−p^)n

The 1/n is brutal: to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. Both results assume independent, identically behaving days. Fat tails and volatility clustering bend that assumption, so treat these numbers as optimistic.

Confidence intervals

A range of plausible values for the hidden parameter, built from your estimate plus or minus about two standard errors.

p^±1.96×SE(p^)

p^=0.523, n = 1,760: SE = √(0.523 × 0.477 / 1760) = 0.0119.

95% CI = 0.523 ± 0.023 = [0.499, 0.546]. It (just) includes 0.5, so the data can't rule out a fair coin.

A 95% CI does not mean "95% chance the true p is in this range". The frequentist meaning: if you repeated the whole study many times, 95% of the intervals you'd build would contain the true p. (The Bayesian version below does give the intuitive meaning.)

Hypothesis tests and p-values

Pick a "nothing is going on" hypothesis. Ask how much worse it explains the data than the best explanation. If it's far worse, reject it.

Null hypothesis: β = 0 (the treatments are equally effective). The log-likelihood at β = 0 was 43.6 units below the maximum, far more than the 1.92 that bounds the confidence interval. The p-value was 9.7 × 10⁻²¹, far below the conventional 0.05, so the null is rejected: combination therapy works better. The trials were so decisive that combination therapy quickly became standard treatment.
Λ=2(ℓ(θ^)−ℓ(θ0))

The formal definition of a p-value: across theoretical repetitions of the data generating process, how often you'd see a test statistic at least as large as the one observed, if the null hypothesis were true. Small p-values mean the data are not consistent with the null.

  • A p-value is not the probability that the null is true.
  • "Not significant" means "not enough evidence", not "no effect".
  • A hypothesis test is a qualitative judgement (effective or not) with uncertainty attached, often more vivid than a number to three significant figures.

Assume the boring explanation (the "null hypothesis") is true, and ask: how surprising is my data? The p-value measures that surprise. Small = surprising = evidence against the boring explanation.

Just above the conventional 0.05 cut-off: weak evidence that up days are more common than down days.

  • A p-value is not the probability that the null is true.
  • "Not significant" doesn't mean "no effect"; it means "not enough evidence".
  • Test 20 things and expect one p < 0.05 by luck alone (the multiple testing trap from Evaluation).

Why inference has stricter rules than prediction

In prediction you can check yourself against held-out answers. In inference there are no answers to check against, so you have to rely on theory to know how wrong you might be.

Risk is the expected (average) loss: how a method performs in general.

  • For prediction, risk is measured empirically: hold out a test set and average the loss.
  • For inference, you never know the true parameter, so risk must come from theory. Two theories exist: frequentist risk (which motivates maximum likelihood) and Bayesian risk (which motivates Bayesian inference).

Both need a probability model. That's why inference is restricted to probabilistic models and two main methods: "the price we pay for a principled approach". In short, inference is constrained because we want to know how far from right we're likely to be.

Causality: say what you mean

Questions about how the world works are usually questions about cause. Proving cause is hard. Randomising is the gold standard; without it you need assumptions, and you should say so.

To read the HIV result causally, three things were needed: principled inference, a model whose parameter has a real-world meaning, and randomisation, which lets a line of causation be drawn from treatment to outcome.

Randomisation isn't always possible or ethical, and much of data science reuses data collected for other purposes. Causal inference is both an attitude and a toolkit. Its strongest messages (lecture 9.3):

  • Be clear about your question. If you want to infer causes, say so.
  • Avoid euphemisms like "association", a slippery word that usually means correlation but sometimes smuggles in causation.
  • Take steps against confounding, mediation and collider bias.
  • Accept that you need assumptions as well as data to infer causation.
Your plan says the project "does not establish causation". That's this advice in action. In the report, write "Brent features improved prediction", never "oil prices drive the basket".

Many tests: Bonferroni correction

Test one thing at the 5% level and you'll be fooled 1 time in 20. Test twelve things and the chance of being fooled at least once is almost a coin flip. So you raise the bar.

1−(1−0.05)12≈0.46

Bonferroni correction: divide the significance threshold by the number of tests.

αeach=αm=0.0512≈0.004

Now you reject a null only when its p-value is below 0.4%. That holds the chance of any false positive across the whole family of tests, the family-wise error rate, at 5%. On a −log₁₀ p scale (bigger = more significant) the threshold is 2.4.

The circular problem that motivates Bayes

To test whether a variable matters, you need to know which other variables to adjust for. But finding out which others matter means testing them too. It goes round in circles.

The frequentist workaround was to include everything, which overfits and loses power. The lecturer calls this the variable selection problem, and "a surprisingly difficult problem" for maximum likelihood and frequentist inference generally.

Bayesian inference handles it naturally by treating "which variables are in the model?" as one more unknown, using Bayesian model averaging. That's the next unit.

In finance: inference is how claims get tested

Where maximum likelihood struggles

Maximum likelihood answers "how often would I be wrong if I repeated this whole study forever?". That's a strange question to build everything on, and it leads to awkward definitions.

In maximum likelihood, the point estimate, the confidence interval and the hypothesis test all depend on the likelihood, plus a confidence level α that controls frequentist risk: the probability of being wrong across theoretical repetitions of the data generating process.

Lecture 9.4 lists the criticisms:

  • Maximum likelihood overfits the parameters to the data, so point estimates are noisier than they need to be.
  • Evidence is judged using hypothetical repetitions that never happened, which is where the awkward definitions of confidence intervals and p-values come from.
  • The circular variable-selection problem from the last unit.

Some statisticians see the Bayesian–frequentist divide as "a schism at the heart of statistics".

The Bayesian idea

Treat the unknown number itself as uncertain, and use probability to describe your belief about it. Start with what you believed before, look at the data, and update.

A parameter like a drug's effectiveness is really fixed. Bayesians treat it as random anyway, because that lets probability represent not just noise in the data but the data scientist's uncertainty. Three objects:

  • Prior distribution: what you assume about the parameter before seeing the data.
  • Likelihood: the same one as before.
  • Posterior distribution: your knowledge after seeing the data.

Named after Thomas Bayes. In one line: Bayesian inference updates prior beliefs in the light of new data. Today's posterior is tomorrow's prior.

The prior is regularisation

Take logs of Bayes' rule and the prior shows up as an extra term added to the log-likelihood: a penalty that pulls estimates towards sensible values. You've met this before, as regularisation.

lnP(θ∣data)=lnP(data∣θ)+lnP(θ)+constant

"The effect of the prior is to add a penalty term to the log-likelihood, and that's a form of regularisation." A Bayesian sees this as a good thing: prior knowledge gives better, less noisy estimates.

The link is exact for the models you know: ridge regression is what you get from a prior saying "coefficients are probably near zero" (a bell-curve prior). The λ in ridge is the strength of that belief. Lasso corresponds to a prior shape that favours exact zeros.

The price: your prior is a choice

Two analysts with the same data but different starting beliefs can reach different conclusions. That's the main objection to Bayesian inference.

The prior is an up-front assumption, "the price we pay for a much simpler and more coherent approach". It makes Bayesian inference fundamentally subjective, although many have tried to define objective priors.

The Bayesian reply: two analysts with different models of the data generating process would also disagree, whichever school they follow. Both priors and models bring subjectivity into inference. The honest response is the same in both cases: state your assumptions.

Small data: why priors (and regularisation) save you

With little data, the raw proportion can be wildly wrong. A sensible prior pulls the estimate towards a reasonable value until the data earns the right to move it.

20 days, 14 up. MLE: 14/20 = 70%. Would you really bet that this basket rises 70% of the time?

With the Beta(50, 50) prior: Beta(64, 56), mean 53%, 95% interval [0.44, 0.62]. Much more sensible.

The link to machine learning: ridge regularisation is mathematically the same as putting a Bayesian prior on the coefficients that says "probably close to zero". The λ "complexity tax" is a statement of prior belief. Lasso corresponds to a different prior shape (one that favours exact zeros).

Bayesian model averaging

Not sure which variables belong in the model? Don't pick one model. Consider all of them, weight each by how believable it is after seeing the data, and average.

"Which variables are in the model?" becomes one more unknown, M, with its own prior. To test whether one variable matters, add up the posterior probabilities of every model that includes it: its posterior inclusion probability.

12 pre-randomisation variables give 2¹² = 4,096 possible models. Prior: each variable has a 1-in-6 chance of mattering, so about 2 of 12 are expected (a binomial prior that penalises complex models: regularisation again).

After the data: the posterior concentrated on two variables. The top model, with prior therapy and the baseline cell count, had 78.5% posterior probability. Posterior inclusion probabilities: 99.9% and 94.3%, up from the prior 16.7%.

The frequentist analysis with Bonferroni found only one. Averaging over models recovered a second signal that "include everything" had drowned.

A sting in the tail: unmeasured confounding

The two variables that mattered pointed the "wrong" way. The likely reason is a hidden common cause that nobody measured.

Both estimated effects were negative: patients with prior therapy, and with higher baseline counts, lost CD4 cells faster. Odd, since therapy should help. The lecturer's conjecture: prior therapy signals a longer, more advanced infection, so it's a marker of worse health, not a cause of it. The data didn't fully capture how advanced each infection was, leaving an unmeasured confounder.

The moral: in observational analysis you can control for confounders you measured, but you must assume there are none you didn't. No method, frequentist or Bayesian, removes that assumption. "Confounding is a pernicious problem in observational studies." The next lecture turns to natural experiments as a way round it.

Markets have the same trap. "Stocks that analysts upgrade go up" could mean upgrades cause rises, or that good news causes both. Finance researchers look for natural experiments (an index rule that forces buying, a regulation that hits some firms and not others) to separate the two.

What your classifier is really estimating

Your logistic regression outputs a conditional probability: the chance of an up day given today's features.

P(Upt+1=1∣xt)

If the model has learned nothing, this is just the base rate (≈ 0.52) every day. Any value it outputs that differs from the base rate is a claim that today's features change the odds. Evaluation then asks whether those claims hold up on unseen days.

Calibration is a useful extra check: of the days where the model said "60% chance of up", were about 60% actually up? A model can rank days well and still be badly calibrated.

In finance: Bayesian thinking is everywhere

Markets give you little data and lots of noise, exactly where a sensible prior helps most.

  • Black–Litterman. A portfolio method that starts from a prior (the returns implied by market prices) and updates it with an investor's views to get posterior expected returns. It exists because raw historical averages are too noisy to trust.
  • Shrinkage. Estimating how hundreds of stocks co-move from limited history is unstable. Shrinkage estimators pull the noisy estimate towards a simple structured target: a prior in all but name.
  • Updating on news. A trader who revises a view as each data release arrives is doing informal Bayesian updating: yesterday's posterior is today's prior.
  • Model averaging. Combining forecasts from several models, weighted by how well each is supported, usually beats picking one "best" model.

Black swans & fat tails

Why history-trained models break exactly when it matters most.

What is a black swan?

A shock almost nobody saw coming, that changes everything, and that people afterwards pretend was obvious.

Named by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (The Black Swan, 2007), after the European belief that all swans were white, until black swans were found in Australia. One observation broke a "law" built on thousands.

A black swan has three properties:

  1. Outlier: nothing in the past convincingly pointed to it.
  2. Extreme impact.
  3. Retrospective predictability: afterwards, everyone builds a story for why it was "obvious".

Related ideas: a grey swan is a known-but-unlikely risk. Taleb himself argued Covid was not a true black swan, because pandemics had been widely warned about. The market reaction, though, was still extreme.

The technical core: fat tails

Huge market crashes and spikes happen far more often than the textbook bell curve says they should.

Many models implicitly assume returns follow a normal (bell-curve) distribution. Real returns have fat tails: extreme days happen far more often than the bell curve allows. The statistic for this is excess kurtosis (normal = 0; daily stock returns are usually well above 0).

The limits of prediction (course Weeks 9–10)

The turkey problem

A turkey is fed every day for 1,000 days. Each day its confidence that the farmer is kind grows. On day 1,001, just before Christmas, it gets its biggest surprise. Its evidence was strongest exactly when it was most wrong.

Philosophers call this the problem of induction (Hume): no number of past observations can prove the future will be like the past. Put the turkey's reasoning into maths with Laplace's "rule of succession", the Bayesian estimate after n successes in n tries:

P(successnexttime)=n+1n+2

After 1,000 good days: 1,001 / 1,002 = 99.9% confident of another good day.

The arithmetic is fine. What failed was the model: it assumed every day was a rerun of the others (replication). The farmer's calendar was a hidden variable the turkey never saw. Your models assume the same thing about markets.

In finance: the tail is the job

Risk management exists because of fat tails. The industry's worst moments came from models that assumed the future would look like a calm past.

  • 19 October 1987 ("Black Monday"). The Dow fell 22.6% in one day. Under a normal distribution with typical volatility, a move that size should essentially never happen in the lifetime of the universe.
  • LTCM, 1998. A hedge fund run by star traders and Nobel laureates relied on historical relationships between bonds. When Russia defaulted, those relationships broke together, and the fund needed a rescue organised by the Federal Reserve.
  • 2007–08. Mortgage models assumed house prices wouldn't fall nationwide at once, because in the data they never had.

How risk is measured:

  • Value at Risk (VaR): "we expect to lose more than £X on only 1 day in 100". It says nothing about how bad that day is.
  • Expected shortfall: the average loss on those worst days. Bank capital rules have moved towards it for exactly that reason.
  • Stress tests: replay 2008 or invent a scenario, and ask what happens. Asking "what if?" instead of "what's likely?".

From brain cells to deep learning

A brain cell is a simple switch: it adds up incoming signals and fires if the total is big enough. Wire up 86 billion of them and you get a mind. Artificial neural networks borrow the idea.

A biological neurone receives signals through branching dendrites. If their sum crosses a threshold, it fires an impulse along its axon, passing signals to other neurones across gaps called synapses.

Activation functions, and why they must bend

The activation function is the "bend" in each neurone. Without a bend, stacking layers achieves nothing: a stack of straight lines is still a straight line.

linear:φ(z)=zsigmoid:φ(z)=11+e−zReLU:φ(z)=max(0,z)
  • Linear: rarely used, for the reason below.
  • Sigmoid (the logistic function): a smooth switch that squashes numbers into 0–1. Its main job is turning numbers into probabilities.
  • ReLU (rectified linear unit): zero below 0, a straight line above. The workhorse of deep learning: simple, fast, and it works.

Why non-linear? Chain two linear neurones:

w2(w1x+b1)+b2=(w2w1)x+(w2b1+b2)

That's just another straight line with a new slope and intercept. Stack as many linear layers as you like and you have rebuilt a linear model, inefficiently.

Layers: the multilayer perceptron

Arrange neurones in layers. Each layer's outputs become the next layer's inputs. Information flows one way, from the features to the prediction.

This is a feed-forward network, also called a multilayer perceptron (MLP). Layers between input and output are hidden layers. Every connection has a weight and every neurone has a bias.

The textbook's tiny network: 2 inputs → 4 neurones → 2 neurones → 1 output.

(2×4 + 4) + (4×2 + 2) + (2×1 + 1) = 12 + 10 + 3 = 25 parameters.

Real networks have millions or billions. That's why neural networks are called parameter-rich and data-hungry.

For you: 7 features → 16 hidden neurones → 1 output would be (7×16 + 16) + (16 + 1) = 145 parameters, for about 1,750 noisy training rows. That's a lot of dials for very little signal.

Training: backpropagation and hill climbing

After each wrong answer, the network works backwards to find how much each weight contributed to the mistake, then nudges every weight slightly in the helpful direction. Repeat thousands of times.

  1. Forward pass: compute the prediction, then the loss.
  2. Backward pass (backpropagation): reverse the flow to find each parameter's gradient: how much would changing it change the loss?
  3. Update: nudge every parameter a little to reduce the loss. Repeat.

This is hill climbing (or, picturing the loss, gradient descent: walking downhill). It's a greedy algorithm: it takes whichever small step looks best right now, so it can end in a local optimum, a nearby valley that isn't the deepest one.

The textbook reran the XOR experiment 20 times from different random starts: with ReLU activations, 14 of 20 starts scored above 95%; the other 6 got stuck at 73–75%. Same data, same network, different luck.
Don't read meaning into individual weights. Two identical networks trained on the same data can end with very different weights yet make the same predictions.

Sometimes the answer depends on a combination. Heating on is comfortable in cold weather and uncomfortable in hot weather. No single straight-line rule captures that.

XOR means "one or the other, but not both". The classes sit in opposite corners, so they are linearly inseparable.

Costs and superpowers

Neural networks can learn almost any pattern, but they need lots of data, lots of tuning, and are overkill for simple problems.

The costs: parameter-rich and data-hungry; fiddly to tune (many hyperparameters); overkill for simple problems, where they can do worse than a simple model.

The five "superpowers":

  1. Smooth predictions, which helps extrapolation beyond the training range. A random forest can only repeat its last prediction (a flat line); a network keeps following the trend. "Better, not magical."
  2. Universal approximation: with enough neurones they can approximate any function.
  3. Efficient with many inputs, compared with rivals such as polynomials.
  4. Trainable in ways that keep overfitting in check.
  5. Immensely flexible: the MLP is only a prototype for richer designs.

Inductive bias: better because of constraints

If you know something about the structure of your data, build it into the network. A network that's forced to respect that structure learns faster from less data.

A plain MLP treats every input as unrelated to every other. Real data has structure: neighbouring pixels belong together; words come in order. Specialising a network to that structure is inductive bias, and it sits behind most of deep learning's big wins.

The specialised networks build an embedding: a compact representation that keeps the signal and discards noise and detail.

Your features are hand-made inductive bias. "20-day volatility" encodes your knowledge that recent turbulence matters; the model doesn't have to discover it from raw prices.

Autoencoders: learn by squeezing

Train a network to copy its input to its output, but force the data through a narrow middle. To get through the squeeze it must learn what's essential.

A bottleneck autoencoder has an encoder that compresses the input to a small embedding (also called the code or latent representation) and a decoder that rebuilds the input from it. The loss is the reconstruction error. Three uses:

  1. Lossy compression: the embedding is a compressed version.
  2. Unsupervised learning: similar items end up close together in the embedding, with no labels given. A two-number embedding of handwritten digits was enough to name the digit 73% of the time.
  3. Anomaly detection: train only on normal examples. Anything it reconstructs badly is unusual. Trained on zeros only, the textbook's autoencoder gave errors nearly five times larger on other digits.
Anomaly detection by "learn normal, flag what doesn't fit" is how much fraud and market-surveillance tooling works: fraud is rare and every case is different, so there are too few labelled examples to learn each kind directly.

CNNs: one small detector, slid everywhere

For images, use one small pattern-detector and slide it across the whole picture, reusing the same weights everywhere. Far fewer dials, and a pattern learned in one corner is recognised in any corner.

  1. A neurone looks at a 3×3 patch of pixels: 9 weights and a bias.
  2. Weight sharing: the neurone for the next patch must use the very same weights. The shared weights are a filter.
  3. The outputs form a feature map.
  4. Max pooling keeps the largest value in each 2×2 tile: half the rows and columns, and less sensitivity to small shifts.
  5. Repeat. Early layers detect edges; deeper layers combine them into textures, shapes and objects.
  6. Global average pooling reduces each map to one number, so any image size works.
  7. Many parallel channels, each with its own filter, give a feature vector that feeds an MLP.

A fully connected layer mapping an 8×8 image to an 8×8 map needs 64×64 + 64 = 4,160 parameters. One 3×3 filter does it with 3×3 + 1 = 10.

A rolling window is the same idea in time: one calculation (say, 20-day volatility) slid along every day of the series.

Transfer learning

A network trained on millions of images learns general skills, like spotting edges, in its early layers. You can keep those layers and train only a small new part for your own task, with far less data.

Trained encoders can be "unplugged" and reused inside different models. Inductive bias, learned representations, embeddings and transfer learning sit at the heart of modern deep learning. The same idea powers language models: pre-train once on huge data, then adapt (next unit).

So why not use one for your project?

Neural networks shine with mountains of richly structured data. You have a small pile of very noisy numbers.

  • Data size: about 1,750 training rows against hundreds of parameters.
  • Noise: daily returns are mostly noise, so flexibility mostly fits noise.
  • Tuning: more hyperparameters means more chances to fool yourself (multiple testing).
  • Explainability: you can read and defend a linear model's coefficients.

The textbook says it directly: using a neural network when a simple model would do "can produce unflattering performance". A sensible extension for the Discussion: an LSTM on return sequences as a comparison, or text embeddings of news headlines.

Language, intelligence and the Turing test

For 70 years computers were brilliant at arithmetic and hopeless at "getting the gist" of language. Then, between 2014 and 2022, that changed.

In 1950 Alan Turing replaced the vague question "can machines think?" with a concrete test based on a parlour game, the imitation game: a judge exchanges typed messages with a hidden person and a hidden machine. If the judge can't reliably tell which is which, the machine passes. Turing called the original question meaningless; the test was something you could actually measure.

Words must become numbers

A computer only handles numbers, so every word needs a number code. The obvious codes carry no meaning at all.

  • Character codes (UTF-8) work below the level of meaning: great, big, huge, large mean similar things but share no spelling, while grate, bog, hog, barge look similar and mean nothing alike.
  • Arbitrary integers (lamb = 19,343) are worse: the model would treat nearby codes as similar meanings.
  • One-hot encoding avoids false similarity: each word is a vector of zeros with a single 1, "a huge multiple-choice box". But with a 30,000-word vocabulary each word is 30,000 numbers, and it says nothing about which words are related.

Tokens. Real models split text into tokens: whole words, parts of words or punctuation. GPT-2 uses about 50,000. Rare words are built from familiar pieces ("Unbelievably" → Un·bel·iev·ably), so nothing is ever out of vocabulary. Each token ID is just a row number in an embedding table.

  3. Re-weight with TF-IDF to favour words common in this story but rare overall.
  4. Normalise so long and short stories are comparable.
  5. Train a classifier on the vectors.

Embeddings: meaning in a hundred numbers

Give each word a short list of numbers, learned so that words used in similar contexts get similar lists. Meaning becomes position in space.

The principle, from the linguist J. R. Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." The recipe (word2vec): train a shallow network to predict a word's neighbour. A revealing surprise: the nearest word to large is small, its opposite. Embeddings don't find synonyms; they find words that fill the same slot in a sentence ("a ___ house"). Think of it as a thesaurus of alternatives.

Averaged GloVe vectors (100 numbers per story) classify news about as well as TF-IDF (4,000 numbers): 87.8% vs 86.8% with 5,000 training stories. With only 250 stories, embeddings win easily (about 82% vs 63%), because they arrive already knowing which words are alike.

RNNs: reading in order, with memory

A recurrent network reads one word at a time, carrying a memory forward. At each step it mixes the new word with what it remembers.

ht=tanh(Wxt+Uht−1+b)
xt
the input at position t (a word embedding)
ht−1
the memory from the previous position (zeros at the start)
W,U
weights, the same at every position (weight sharing along the sequence)

They forget. The further along the sequence, the harder it is to remember early words: the vanishing gradient problem. Multiply a number below 1 by itself enough times and it disappears (0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005). LSTM and GRU units add gates that choose what to remember and forget. They help, but long documents still fade.

They only know the whole context at the end. In "Mary had a little lamb … with mint sauce", the crucial word comes last. A bidirectional RNN reads both ways so every position sees the full context.

Bidirectional reading is fine for translation, where you have the whole sentence. For forecasting it would be leakage: reading backwards from the future.

Encoder, decoder and the context vector

To translate, one network reads the English sentence and squeezes it into a single summary. A second network writes the French sentence from that summary, one word at a time.

  • Sequence-to-vector: an RNN encoder feeds a fixed-size context vector into an MLP, for classification (spam, sentiment, news topic).
  • Sequence-to-sequence: a bidirectional encoder reads the source; a one-directional decoder generates the translation, consulting the context vector at every step.

Ambiguity is why context matters: "Mary had a little lamb" changes meaning if it continues "with mint sauce", and French uses a different verb for to have and to eat.

Two bottlenecks, and attention

The old translators had two problems: everything had to squeeze through one fixed-size summary, and words had to be processed one at a time. Attention fixed the first.

  1. Information bottleneck: the whole input passes through one context vector, a "dumb average" that's the same at every step of decoding.
  2. Computational bottleneck: sequential processing blocks parallel computing.

Attention (Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio, 2014) replaces the plain average with a weighted average, where the weights depend on what the decoder is producing right now. To write "agneau" it puts most weight on the encoder's memory of "lamb".

αt,i=exp(score(st,hi))∑jexp(score(st,hj))ct=∑i=1nαt,ihi
st
the decoder's state at step t (what it's "asking about")
hi
the encoder's memory of input word i
αt,i
attention weights: positive, summing to 1 (a softmax)
ct
the context for this step: a weighted average

Softmax of scores [2, 1, 0]: e² = 7.39, e¹ = 2.72, e⁰ = 1; total 11.11 → weights [0.67, 0.24, 0.09].

Later the weights were reinterpreted as a lookup: a query is matched against keys to decide how much of each value to return. Self-attention applies this within one sequence: every word looks at every other word.

The transformer (2017)

"Attention Is All You Need": throw away the one-word-at-a-time reading and let every word look at every other word at once. That fixed the second bottleneck and made training on vast data possible.

Kind of attentionWhereWhat looks at what
Self-attentionEncoderEvery source word looks at every other, both directions
Masked self-attentionDecoderEach word looks only at earlier words, so it can't cheat by peeking ahead
Cross-attentionDecoder → encoderEach target word picks out the relevant source words
  • No built-in sense of order: position is added to the embeddings.
  • Each block = an attention sublayer (mix information across words) + a feed-forward sublayer (process each word). Most of the weights, and perhaps most of the "world knowledge", live in the feed-forward parts.
  • Parallel: all positions are computed together on GPUs, so training is fast.
  • Generating is still sequential: one token at a time, which is why chatbot text appears like a typewriter.
The causal mask is the transformer's leakage guard: during training it hides the future so the model can't use words it's supposed to predict. Your "availability, not date" rule does the same job for oil prices.

BERT and GPT: two ways to use a transformer

BERT keeps the reading half and is good at understanding text. GPT keeps the writing half and is good at producing it.

Both are foundation models in the pre-train, fine-tune paradigm: train once on huge unlabelled text (self-supervised: the text supplies its own answers), then adapt to a task with a small labelled set. That's transfer learning for language.

BERT gives the same word different vectors in different contexts: "bank" by a river and "bank" with money end up far apart. In 2019 Google said BERT had delivered the biggest improvement to search in five years.

FinBERT is BERT fine-tuned on financial text to classify sentiment in news and earnings calls. It's the standard example of a foundation model adapted to finance, and the kind of feature a "news sentiment" extension to your project would use.

How a language model writes

Given the text so far, the model produces a probability for every possible next token. It picks one, adds it to the text, and repeats. That is all.

P(wt∣w1,w2,…,wt−1)
  1. Compute a probability for every token in the vocabulary.
  2. Draw one at random in proportion to its probability (so the same prompt can give different replies).
  3. Append it; the longer text becomes the new prompt. Repeat until an end token.

This is the autoregressive loop. The model has no separate plan for the whole reply.

Scaling. GPT-2 (2019), trained on about ten times more data than GPT-1, wrote fluent text with no fine-tuning (zero-shot). The scaling hypothesis: prediction improves predictably with more data and bigger models. GPT-1 saw about 4 billion tokens; GPT-3 (2020) several hundred billion, at a cost of perhaps $10 million and around 500 tonnes of CO₂.

Next-token prediction is forecasting, like yours: predict the next item from the past. The difference is scale and signal. Language is highly predictable from context; tomorrow's return is almost not.

From GPT to ChatGPT, and is it intelligent?

Raw models trained on the internet picked up its biases and nastiness. Human feedback was used to make them helpful and safe, and the result was ChatGPT.

  • RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback): people rated outputs, rewarding helpful, coherent answers and penalising harmful or dishonest ones. The ratings were used to fine-tune the model's weights.
  • ChatGPT (November 2022) put it in everyone's hands.
  • Hallucination: a fluent, confident output that is false or invented. Models produce statistically plausible text, not checked facts.

Today the Turing test looks like something a chatbot could pass. Yet chatbots hallucinate, fail simple logic puzzles and have no goals or experiences. Whether that is intelligence or a sophisticated imitation is openly debated; the lecture leaves it as a question for you.

You are accountable for your conclusions

A model doesn't take responsibility; the person who built and interpreted it does. "The algorithm said so" is never a good enough reason.

Automation bias

People tend to trust a computer's answer more than they should, even when their own judgement says otherwise, especially when the answer arrives with confident-looking decimals.

  • A model printing "p(up) = 0.563" feels precise. Its real uncertainty (Probability unit) might be ±5 points.

  • Generative AI makes this sharper: chatbots "hallucinate", producing fluent, confident, false statements (textbook Week 6).

Guard against it in your write-up: report uncertainty, compare to baselines, and say plainly when results are no better than chance.

Why traders think in expected value

A bet is good if, played many times, it makes money on average. That long-run average is called the expected value (EV).

A trading firm is a machine for finding lots of small positive-EV bets and making them over and over. Any single trade can lose. The average is what pays the bills. That's why trading interviews keep coming back to one skill: put a number on something uncertain, fast, then decide whether to trade.

Linearity: the shortcut behind half of all puzzles

The average of a total is the total of the averages. Always. Even when the parts depend on each other.

The trick: turn "how many…?" questions into a sum of indicators. An indicator is 1 if something happens and 0 if not, so its EV is just the probability it happens. Then add them up.

Variance: the average isn't the whole story

Two bets can have the same average and feel completely different. Variance measures how spread out the results are.

Var(X)=E[X2]−(E[X])2sd=Var(X)

"Would you play?" Working backwards from the last decision

When a game has choices, solve the last choice first, then step back. This is called backward induction.

One reroll. Roll a die; you may roll once more and must take the second roll. You're paid the final number. What's the game worth?

If you reroll, you expect 3.5. So keep 4, 5 or 6 (average 5) and reroll 1, 2 or 3.

EV = ½ × 5 + ½ × 3.5 = 4.25.

Now you get up to two rerolls (three rolls at most). What's the game worth?

When EV breaks: the St Petersburg paradox

Flip a coin until it lands heads. If that takes k flips you win £2k. Each outcome contributes (½)k × 2k = £1, and there are infinitely many outcomes, so the EV is infinite. Yet nobody would pay £1,000 to play.

  • Nobody can pay you infinity. If the house holds about £1bn (230), every outcome past 30 flips pays the same, and the EV drops to about £31.
  • Money matters less the more you have. Valuing outcomes by log(wealth) instead of wealth (Bernoulli, 1738) gives a small, sensible price. Log utility returns in the Kelly unit.

Desks maximise EV subject to risk limits (VaR, stress losses, position caps). A positive-EV bet that can bankrupt you is a bad bet, because after ruin you can't play the repeated game that EV is about.

Symmetry: the quant's favourite shortcut

If two outcomes are mirror images of each other, they have the same probability. Spotting the mirror often skips all the algebra.

First ace. Where does the first ace appear in a shuffled deck, on average? The 4 aces split the 48 other cards into 5 gaps of equal expected size: 48/5 = 9.6 cards. So the first ace is card 10.6 on average.

Adverse selection: the trader who knows more

The people most eager to trade with you are often the ones who know something you don't. Your spread is the fee that pays for those losses.

A simplified Glosten–Milgrom model. An asset is worth 100 or 110, equally likely. A share π of traders know the true value: they buy if it's 110 and sell if it's 100. Everyone else buys or sells at random. To break even, you set your ask equal to the asset's expected value given that someone wants to buy.

A buy is more likely when V = 110: P(V = 110 | buy) = (1 + π)/2. So E[V | buy] = 100 + 10 × (1 + π)/2 = 105 + 5π.

With 20% informed traders, you quote 104 at 106. With no informed traders, you could quote 105 at 105.

This is why spreads widen before earnings and OPEC meetings (more informed traders), and why they're razor-thin on huge, liquid names like index futures.

Position sizing and the Kelly criterion

Having an edge is half the job. Betting the right amount is the other half, and getting it wrong can turn a winning edge into ruin.

Edge isn't enough: how much should you bet?

A coin lands heads 60% of the time and pays even money. You start with £100 and get 100 flips. How much of your money should you stake each time?

  • Bet everything each flip: one tails and you're bust. Over 100 flips, ruin is almost certain, despite the edge.
  • Bet 1%: you'll very likely grow, but slowly.
  • Bet 20%: the fastest long-run growth. This is the Kelly fraction.

The key is that wealth compounds. What matters is the average of the log of your returns, not the average return itself.

Stationarity: why you model returns, not prices

A series is stationary if its behaviour (average, spread, memory) doesn't drift over time. Prices wander off; returns roughly don't.

Spurious regression (Granger and Newbold, 1974): regress one random walk on another, completely unrelated one, and you'll often get a big R² and a "highly significant" t-statistic. Two trending things always look related.

np.corrcoef(np.diff(a), np.diff(b))[0, 1]           # about 0: the honest answer

That's why every feature in your plan is a return, a volatility or a ratio, never a raw price level. Say so in your wrangling section: it's a deliberate choice.

Monday's 5-day target and Tuesday's share four of the same days. So their errors are linked, and standard formulas think you have more independent evidence than you do.

def newey_west_var(x, lags):
    x = np.asarray(x) - np.mean(x)
    n = len(x)
    v = x @ x / n

Testing many ideas: Bonferroni, Holm and FDR

Bonferroni (from your Week 9 lecture) controls the chance of any false positive, but becomes very strict as the number of tests grows. Alternatives:

  • Holm: like Bonferroni but step-by-step, so it's always at least as powerful.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg: controls the false discovery rate, the expected share of your "discoveries" that are flukes. That's often the right target in research.

BH procedure at 5%: sort the m p-values from smallest to largest. Find the largest k with p(k) ≤ (k/m) × 0.05. Call the first k tests discoveries.

Calls and puts in one picture

A call is the right, but not the obligation, to buy at a fixed price (the strike) on a set date. A put is the right to sell. You pay a premium up front for that right.

call=max(ST−K,0)put=max(K−ST,0)

A Shell call with strike 2,500p. At expiry Shell is 2,620p: the call pays 120p. At 2,400p it pays 0, and you've lost only the premium.

Options let you bet on (or insure against) size of move, not just direction. Buying both a call and a put at the same strike (a straddle) pays off if the price moves a lot either way. It's a pure bet on volatility.

That's the bridge to your project: a straddle is a bet on your second target.

Put–call parity: the no-arbitrage backbone

Owning a call and selling a put at the same strike has exactly the same payoff as owning the stock and borrowing the strike price. Identical payoffs must have identical prices, or someone gets free money.

C−P=S−Ke−rT

S = 100, K = 100, r = 0. If the call is 6, the put must also be 6. If the put trades at 5: buy the put, sell the call, buy the stock. That locks in a riskless 1.

S = 105, K = 100, r = 0, call = 8. What must the put cost?

P = C − S + K = 8 − 105 + 100 = 3.

def load_real(shel_path, bp_path, brent_path):
    def read(p):
        d = pd.read_csv(p, parse_dates=["Date"]).set_index("Date").sort_index()
        assert d.index.is_unique, f"duplicate dates in {p}"
        return d
    shel, bp, brent = read(shel_path), read(bp_path), read(brent_path)
    if "available_time" in brent:
        brent["available_time"] = pd.to_datetime(brent["available_time"], utc=True).dt.tz_convert("Europe/London")
    else:   # conservative: usable only from 08:00 the next day
        brent["available_time"] = (brent.index + pd.Timedelta(days=1, hours=8)).tz_localize("Europe/London")
    return shel, bp, brent

Expected columns: Date, AdjClose, Volume for the shares and Date, price (plus available_time if you have it) for Brent. Rename LSEG's field names to these when you export.

The checks

def data_checks(shel, bp, brent):
    out = {}
    for name, d, col in [("shel", shel, "AdjClose"), ("bp", bp, "AdjClose"), ("brent", brent, "price")]:
        r = d[col].pct_change()
        out[name] = dict(rows=len(d), first=str(d.index.min().date()), last=str(d.index.max().date()),
                         missing=int(d[col].isna().sum()), non_positive=int((d[col] <= 0).sum()),
                         jumps_over_15pct=[str(x.date()) for x in r.index[r.abs() > 0.15]])
    return out
  • Jumps over 15% are either real events (March 2020) or data problems: an unadjusted dividend, pence vs pounds, or a ticker change. Investigate every one.
  • Shell's history: its A and B shares were unified in January 2022 and the London ticker became SHEL. Check that LSEG gives you one continuous adjusted series across that date.
  • Pence vs pounds: London prices are usually quoted in pence (GBX). A 100× jump means a unit switch.
Never "fix" the raw CSV by hand. Fix things in code so the fix is visible, repeatable and explainable: evidence vs inference.

The rows, date range, missing counts and any flagged jumps are your data generating process paragraph's facts.

Lab 2 · Line up the clocks

Join Brent by when it was published, not by its date label. This lab is where most finance projects quietly leak.

Build track · uses Asking questions of data (leakage) and the as-of join

Goal

Each forecast, made just after the London close, sees only the Brent value that had already been published by then.

A Brent value labelled "Monday" may be published Monday evening, after your Monday forecast. Joining on the date would hand your model a number from its own future.

The as-of join

def attach_brent(df, brent):
    b = brent.copy()
    b["brent_ret"] = b["price"].pct_change()
    b["brent_vol_5"] = b["brent_ret"].rolling(5).std()
    b = b.dropna(subset=["brent_ret"])[["available_time", "brent_ret", "brent_vol_5"]].sort_values("available_time")
    df = df.reset_index()
    df["forecast_time"] = (df["Date"] + FORECAST_CLOCK).dt.tz_localize("Europe/London")
    return pd.merge_asof(df.sort_values("forecast_time"), b,
                         left_on="forecast_time", right_on="available_time",
                         direction="backward",            # latest value BEFORE the forecast
                         allow_exact_matches=False)       # strictly before
  • Both sides must be sorted by their time key, or merge_asof raises an error.
  • Both times are timezone-aware (Europe/London), so daylight saving can't shift things by an hour.

On a Monday, Brent is published at 18:00 and your forecast clock is 16:40. Which Brent return does Monday's row get?

The as-of join looks backward from 16:40 Monday and finds Friday's value, published Friday evening.

One sentence in wrangling: "Brent was joined to each forecast by publication time using an as-of join, so only information available at 16:40 London time was used."

def basket_returns(shel, bp):
    px = pd.DataFrame({"shel": shel["AdjClose"], "bp": bp["AdjClose"]}).dropna()
    rets = px.pct_change()
    return 0.5 * rets["shel"] + 0.5 * rets["bp"]        # daily-rebalanced 50/50
  • .dropna() after combining keeps only days when both traded.
  • Simple returns average correctly across assets (a basket's return is the weighted average). Log returns add correctly across time. That's why the basket is built from simple returns.
  • Fixed 50/50 weights every day imply daily rebalancing. Buy-and-hold would let the winner's weight grow.

Lab 5 · Targets without peeking

Build y from the future and X from the past, and make the boundary between them impossible to cross by accident.

Build track · uses Features and targets, and Asking questions of data

Goal

Two targets per row: tomorrow's direction and the volatility of the next 5 days. Each target is stamped with the date it ends, so splits can be purged.

nxt = r.shift(-1)                                         # tomorrow's return, on today's row
df["r_next"] = nxt
df["y_dir"] = np.where(nxt.isna(), np.nan, (nxt > 0).astype(float))
df["y_vol"] = r.rolling(H).std().shift(-H)                # std of r[t+1 .. t+5]
df["target_end"] = pd.Series(df.index, index=df.index).shift(-H)

Why does rolling(5).std().shift(-5) give days t+1 to t+5? On row t+5, the rolling window covers t+1 … t+5. Shifting by −5 moves that value up to row t.

  • np.where(nxt.isna(), np.nan, …) stops the last day becoming "down" (NaN > 0 is False).
  • shift(-k) (negative) appears only in target code. If you ever see it in feature code, that's leakage.

Which line would leak?

shift(−1) on a feature pulls tomorrow's window into today's row.

The single most valuable test in the project: delete the future and check nothing in the past changes.

Build track · uses Asking questions of data and Testing without fooling yourself

Goal

Automated proof that no feature uses future information, run every time the script runs.

def leakage_checks(df, shel, bp, brent, cut="2020-06-30"):
    has = df["available_time"].notna()
    assert (df.loc[has, "available_time"] < df.loc[has, "forecast_time"]).all(), "Brent from the future!"
    short = build(shel[:cut], bp[:cut], brent[:cut])          # rebuild with the future deleted
    a = df.set_index("Date").loc[:cut, FEATS]
    b = short.set_index("Date").loc[:cut, FEATS]
    assert np.allclose(a.values, b.values, equal_nan=True), "a feature changed when the future was deleted"
    return True

The truncation test is the clever bit. If any feature on 30 June 2020 changes when everything after that date is deleted, that feature was looking at the future. It catches mistakes you'd never spot by reading code: a stray bfill(), a centred rolling window, a scaler fitted on all the data.

You add df["vol_20"] = r.rolling(20, center=True).std(). What happens?

center=True puts the window around day t, half of it in the future. The test catches it.

def clf_candidates():
    c = {f"logistic C={C}": Pipeline([("scale", StandardScaler()),
                                      ("m", LogisticRegression(C=C, max_iter=2000))]) for C in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0)}
    for depth, leaf in [(3, 50), (5, 20)]:
        c[f"forest d={depth} leaf={leaf}"] = RandomForestClassifier(
            n_estimators=300, max_depth=depth, min_samples_leaf=leaf, random_state=SEED, n_jobs=-1)
    return c

def tune(cands, tr, va, feats, target, score):
    best = {}
    for name, m in cands.items():
        m.fit(tr[feats], tr[target])
        s = score(m, va[feats], va[target])            # log loss (direction) or MAE (vol)
        f = family(name)
        if f not in best or s < best[f][1]:
            best[f] = (name, s)
    return best
  • Pipelines fit the scaler on training rows only. No leakage from scaling.
  • Direction is tuned on log loss, not accuracy. Log loss uses the full probability, so it's far less noisy when everything sits near 50%.
  • After choosing, refit on train + validation (2015–2023) and predict 2024–25 once.
  • Five candidates per target, declared in code: a small, honest search. Write the grid in your methods section.

Three lenses on "does Brent matter?"

Ask the same question three ways. Where they agree, you can be confident. Where they disagree, you have something interesting to discuss.

LensQuestionUnit
Ablation (prediction)Does test error rise when Brent is removed and the model retrained?
Likelihood ratio test (frequentist)Is Brent's coefficient zero?
Bayesian model averagingHow much posterior weight sits on models that include Brent?
What happened on simulated data (where a small true Brent effect was built in): test MAE was identical with and without Brent; the likelihood ratio test gave p = 0.035, "significant"; Bayesian model averaging gave Brent an inclusion probability of only 0.03. Three lenses, three different impressions of one tiny effect. A tiny effect can be statistically detectable yet useless for prediction, and a sceptical prior can outweigh a borderline p-value. Drill · Markets

The dice market

Three dice are rolled in secret. You make a market on their sum, three times, as the dice are revealed one by one. An informed trader has always seen one more die than you. A noise trader trades at random.

How the counterparties behave

Informed trader: knows one more die than you. They buy from you if their expected sum is above your ask, and sell to you if it's below your bid.

Noise trader: 60% of the time trades one lot, buying or selling at random.

Width limits: at most 4 before any die is shown, 3 after one, 2 after two, as an interviewer would demand.

At the end, each lot you bought makes (sum − bid) and each lot you sold makes (ask − sum).
