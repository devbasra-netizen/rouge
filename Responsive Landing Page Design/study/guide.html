Your whole project as a story

No jargon. Read this first, and every other unit will make more sense.

Chapter 1 · The money

Imagine you've got £5,000. You put half into Shell and half into BP, two giant oil companies listed in London. That pair of shares is your basket.

Every day the basket's value moves a little. Some days up, some days down. Your project is about guessing those moves before they happen.

Chapter 2 · The hunch

Shell and BP make most of their money from oil. So when the price of oil jumps (say, because of a war or a big announcement), their shares often react.

Your hunch: if I know what oil and the shares did recently, can I make a better guess about what the shares do next?

Chapter 3 · Two questions

You're asking the computer two things about each day:

  1. "Will my basket go up tomorrow?" Yes or no.
  2. "How bumpy will the next week be?" Calm, or a rollercoaster?

The second one sounds harder, but it's actually easier: bumpy weeks tend to come in clusters, like bad weather. Whether it goes up tomorrow is close to a coin flip.

Chapter 4 · How the computer learns

You have about 10 years of history, roughly 2,700 trading days. For each old day you know two things: what the clues looked like (recent moves, how busy trading was, what oil did) and what actually happened next.

You show the computer thousands of these "clues → what happened" examples. It looks for a rule that links them. That's all "machine learning" means here: finding a pattern in examples where you already know the answer.

Chapter 5 · Simple vs clever

You try two kinds of rule-finder for each question: a simple one (a straight-line rule, easy to explain) and a clever one (a "random forest": hundreds of mini decision-flowcharts that vote).

You also try each one with oil data and without it. If adding oil makes the guesses better, that answers your hunch.

Chapter 6 · The fair test

The computer learns from 2015–2021, you fine-tune using 2022–2023, and the final exam is 2024–2025, years it has never seen. Just like real life, where you can only learn from the past.

The big danger is accidental cheating: letting a tiny bit of "tomorrow" sneak into today's clues. For example, using an oil price that was only published after you made your guess. It makes your results look amazing, but they'd be fake. Most of your plan is about preventing this.

Chapter 7 · The lazy guess

To know if your model is any good, you compare it to a lazy guess: "it'll go up, because it usually does" or "next week will be as bumpy as last week". If your clever model can't beat the lazy guess, it hasn't learned anything useful.

Chapter 8 · The surprises

Markets have sudden shocks nobody saw coming: Covid in 2020, the Ukraine war in 2022. These are called black swans. Your models can't predict them, because they learn from the past and these were new.

That's fine. You just need to say so honestly, look at how your models behaved during those crazy periods, and not claim more than your results show.

Unit 1

What data science actually is

The mental model everything else hangs on.

Data science = question → data → model → honest evaluation

Data science is using old information to make a smart guess about something you don't know yet, then checking honestly whether your guesses were any good.

Most of the work is not the clever model. It is asking a question the data can actually answer, making the data trustworthy, and then testing the model in a way that can't fool you.

A weather forecaster. They use yesterday's pressure, temperature and wind (clues) to guess tomorrow's rain (answer). They're judged on many future days, not on how well they "explain" last year.
Your plan asks: can historical returns, volume and Brent moves predict next-day direction and next-five-day volatility, and does adding Brent help?

Supervised vs unsupervised

Supervised means you learn with the answers at the back of the book. Unsupervised means you get no answers and just look for patterns. You have the answers (you know what the shares did next), so yours is supervised.

Supervised: you have examples with the right answer attached. "Here were Monday's clues; Tuesday's return was positive." The model learns to map clues to answers.

Unsupervised: no answer column. The model looks for structure on its own, e.g. grouping stocks that move together.

Yours is supervised, because for every historical day you know what happened next.

Classification vs regression

Classification answers a yes/no or pick-a-box question ("will it go up?"). Regression answers a how-much question ("how much will it wobble?"). You're doing one of each.

X is everything you show the computer (the clues). y is the thing you want it to guess (the answer). Each day is one row: clues on the left, answer on the right.

Finance foundations

The market mechanics behind every number in your dataset.

The basket and equal weighting

Imagine splitting £5,000 evenly between two shops' shares. Same money in each, even though one share costs £25 and the other £5, so you end up with a different number of each.

A basket is a set of holdings. Its value = sum of (shares × price). 50/50 means equal money, not equal numbers of shares.

Try it: build a 50/50 basket

Basket index: instead of pounds, start at 100. A +1% day takes it to 101. This charts performance without pretending you hold a real tradable product.

Bid, ask, spread, mid

Like a currency exchange booth: it buys from you at one price and sells to you at a slightly higher price. The gap is how it makes money. The middle is the "fair" price.

  • Bid: the price someone will pay to buy from you.
  • Ask (offer): the price someone will accept to sell to you.
  • Spread = ask − bid. The cost of trading immediately.
  • Mid = (bid + ask) / 2. A fair "value" estimate.
Try it: cost to buy vs sell the basket

Holding 100 Shell + 500 BP

You explicitly say you do not assess "executable bid/ask pricing". That's arithmetic, not machine learning, and would widen scope. Good scoping.

Returns: the unit of everything

A return is how much something went up or down as a percentage. Using percentages lets you compare a £25 share and a £5 share fairly.

return = (new price − old price) / old price

£25 → £25.50 = 0.02 = 2%. Returns let you compare a £25 share with a £5 share fairly, and they're much closer to "stable" than prices, which trend.

basket return = 0.5 × Shell return + 0.5 × BP return
Try it

Multi-day (compounded): 5-day return = (1+r₁)(1+r₂)…(1+r₅) − 1. Not just a sum.

Log returns ln(P₁/P₀) are an alternative that add up over time. Simple returns are fine for your plan.

Daily rebalancing

Every evening you top up or trim each share so it's back to exactly half and half. Real life would charge fees for that; you're ignoring fees on purpose.

If Shell rises and BP falls, you now hold more than 50% in Shell. Using 0.5/0.5 weights every day assumes you trade back to 50/50 each evening. That's daily rebalancing.

Real trading would cost spread + commission each time. Your plan says "theoretical daily rebalancing before costs", which is the honest way to state it.

Adjusted prices

When a company pays out cash to shareholders (a dividend), its share price drops a bit, but nobody actually lost money. "Adjusted" prices fix the history so these drops don't look like losses.

When a company pays a dividend, the share price drops by roughly that amount on the ex-dividend date. Shareholders didn't lose money, they got cash. A raw price series would show a fake "loss".

Adjusted prices rewrite history to remove these jumps (dividends, splits, rights issues), so returns reflect what an investor really earned.

Mixing adjusted and unadjusted series, or adjusting one stock but not the other. Use the same adjustment method for both.

Shell's share history

Shell used to have two types of shares. In 2022 they merged into one. Your data spans that change, so you have to check the price history joins up properly.

Until January 2022, Shell (then Royal Dutch Shell) had two share lines, A and B shares (old tickers RDSA / RDSB). They were merged into one line and the company became Shell plc, ticker SHEL.

Your data covers 2015–2025, so it crosses this change. You need to check LSEG gives you one continuous, consistent series, or decide which old line to splice from.

"Checks will cover … Shell's historical share-class changes." This is exactly what that means.

Pence vs pounds

London share prices are often shown in pence. "2,500" means £25. Mix the two and your numbers are 100 times off.

London shares are usually quoted in pence (GBX), not pounds. Shell at "2,500" means £25.00. If one source gives pence and another pounds, returns stay correct (ratios cancel) but any value calculation will be off by 100×.

Brent: spot vs futures

Brent is the main price tag for oil. "Spot" is today's price. "Futures" are deals to buy oil later, and they expire, which makes their price history jumpy and harder to use.

Brent is the benchmark price for North Sea crude oil, used to price most of the world's oil.

  • Spot (e.g. Dated Brent): price for physical oil now-ish. One continuous number per day.
  • Futures: contracts to buy oil on a future date. Each contract expires, so a continuous series has to roll from one contract to the next, which can create artificial jumps.

Spot is the simpler starting point, but check when each day's value is published (Unit 3).

Volatility = how bumpy

Volatility is how much the price jumps around, not which way it goes. A rollercoaster that ends where it started was still very bumpy.

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns: how spread out daily moves are. It says nothing about direction.

σ = √( Σ (rᵢ − r̄)² / (n − 1) )
Same ending, very different volatility

Both paths start at 100 and end at ~100. The amber one would have a much higher 5-day volatility.

A forecast of "1.4% volatility" ≠ "1.4% gain". It means typical daily moves of about ±1.4%. Annualised ≈ daily × √252 ≈ 22%.

With only 5 days, the standard deviation is very noisy. One big day dominates it. That's why your plan says "noisy five-day volatility estimates favour restrained model complexity".

Volume and relative volume

Volume is how many shares changed hands that day. Relative volume asks: is today busier than usual? A value of 2 means twice as busy as normal.

Volume = number of shares traded that day. Raw volume isn't comparable across years, so you use relative volume:

relative volume = today's volume / average of last 20 days

1.0 = normal day. 2.5 = something's happening (news, index rebalance, results).

Bias in your data

Bias means your data gives a lopsided picture. You only picked two big oil firms that are still alive, so your results might not apply to smaller or failed ones.

  • Selection bias: you chose 2 companies. Shell and BP are giant, integrated, London-listed. Results may not apply to other oil firms.
  • Survivorship bias: you picked firms that still exist. Oil companies that went bust in 2015–2020 are invisible, which makes the sector look more resilient than it was.
  • Period bias: 2015–2025 includes an oil crash, Covid, and an energy crisis. Relationships may be specific to those regimes.
  • Currency exposure: oil is priced in US dollars, the shares in pounds. Some co-movement is really GBP/USD.
Your final form mentions selection bias but dropped "survivorship". Adding the word back costs one word and shows you know the difference.

Data wrangling checklist

Wrangling is tidying the data before you use it: no repeated days, no mystery gaps, the right units, and both datasets lined up properly.

  3. Weekends and bank holidays are not missing. The exchange was closed.
  4. Units: pence vs pounds. Currency: USD (Brent) vs GBP (shares).
  5. Verify adjustments for dividends and splits.
  6. Instrument history (Shell A/B).

The timing trap (most important idea in this unit)

Only use information you could actually have known at the moment you made the guess. Using anything published later is cheating, even by accident.

Your forecast is made at the London close on day t. You may only use information that existed at that moment.

A Brent value labelled "Monday" might be assessed around the London close and published after it. Join on date alone and Monday's forecast secretly uses Monday-evening oil information. That's lookahead bias, a form of leakage, and it makes results look better than reality.

Betting on a football match after reading the half-time score, then calling yourself a great predictor.

Fix: store when each observation became available, then use an as-of join: for each forecast time, take the most recent oil value published before it. In pandas: pd.merge_asof(..., direction="backward"). The simplest safe version: use yesterday's Brent.

"Brent observations will be aligned using information availability" and "align oil observations by availability rather than date alone".

The form flags linking datasets as "for experienced users". You're linking two: stock prices/volumes and Brent. Your strategy is the as-of join above. Say it clearly; it shows you understand the hardest part.

Exploratory data analysis (EDA)

Before building anything, look at your data with charts. It's like walking round a house before buying it.

Look before you model: plot prices, returns, volume and rolling volatility over time; draw histograms of returns (a distribution shows how often each size of move happens); check correlation between Shell, BP and Brent.

Lags and rolling windows

A lag is just yesterday's (or last week's) number. A rolling window is "the last 5 days", recalculated every day as time moves on.

A lag is an earlier value: a 1-day lag of return is yesterday's return. A rolling window slides forward one day at a time; 20-day volatility always uses the latest 20 trading days.

Why the volatility target is more predictable

Bumpy weeks tend to follow bumpy weeks, so bumpiness is quite guessable. Whether the price goes up tomorrow is close to a coin flip, so don't expect much there.

Markets show volatility clustering: calm days follow calm days, wild days follow wild days. So recent volatility is a genuinely useful clue for future volatility.

Direction is much harder. If tomorrow's direction were easy to predict, traders would already have traded it away. Expect direction accuracy close to 50–55%. That's normal, not failure.

Building the two targets

For each day, the answer is about the future: did it go up tomorrow? How bumpy were the next 5 days? The clues are only about the past.

Direction target (row t): 1 if the basket return on day t+1 is positive, else 0.

y_dir[t] = 1 if r[t+1] > 0 else 0

Volatility target (row t): std dev of returns on days t+1 … t+5.

y_vol[t] = std(r[t+1], …, r[t+5])

The targets look forward; the features look backward. Row t never contains anything from t+1 in X.

The last row has no "tomorrow". In pandas, (r.shift(-1) > 0) turns that missing value into False = 0, a fake "down day". Drop those rows explicitly.

Linear regression

Draw the best straight-line rule through the data: "start at this number, add a bit for each clue". Simple and easy to explain.

Predicts a number as a starting level plus weighted contributions from each feature:

ŷ = b₀ + b₁·vol_5 + b₂·vol_20 + b₃·brent_ret + …

Training finds the coefficients (b's) that minimise squared error on the training data. Easy to read: b₁ tells you how much predicted volatility rises per unit of recent volatility.

A straight line can predict negative volatility, which is impossible. Your plan sets those to zero, decided in advance. That's the right move (np.clip(pred, 0, None)).

Logistic regression

Same idea as linear regression, but the answer is squeezed into a percentage chance between 0% and 100%, like "56% chance it goes up".

Despite the name, it's for classification. It builds a linear score z, then squashes it into a probability between 0 and 1 with the sigmoid:

p = 1 / (1 + e^(−z))
Drag the score

With a 50% threshold, 56% → predict "up". The 56% is an estimate, not a guarantee.

Decision tree

A game of 20 Questions: "Was last month bumpy? Was today busy?" Each answer leads down a branch to a guess.

A flowchart of yes/no questions learned from data:

vol_20 > 1.8%? ├─ yes → relvol > 1.5? ─ yes → predict 2.4% vol │ └ no → predict 1.9% └─ no → predict 1.1%

The tree picks the questions and thresholds that best split the training data. Deep trees memorise noise, so you cap depth.

Random forest

Ask hundreds of slightly different decision trees and go with the group's answer. A crowd usually beats one person.

Hundreds of trees, each trained on a random resample of rows (bootstrap) and allowed to consider a random subset of features at each split. Classification: trees vote. Regression: trees average.

Asking 300 slightly different analysts and averaging their answers. Individual quirks cancel out.

Strength: captures interactions (e.g. "high volume matters only when volatility is already high") that a linear model misses. Weakness: can still overfit; harder to interpret.

Parameters, hyperparameters, regularisation

Parameters are what the computer learns. Hyperparameters are the settings you choose. Regularisation is a rule that stops the model getting too complicated.

  • Parameter: learned from data (a regression coefficient, a tree's split threshold).
  • Hyperparameter: a setting you choose (max tree depth, number of trees, regularisation strength C).
  • Regularisation: a penalty that discourages complexity, e.g. shrinking large coefficients. In trees: max_depth, min_samples_leaf.

Choose hyperparameters using the validation set, never the test set.

"Limited tuning" and "restrained model complexity" are regularisation choices.

Scaling (standardisation)

Putting all clues on the same scale so a big-number clue doesn't drown out a small-number one. Like converting everything to the same currency before comparing.

Features live on different scales (returns ~0.01, relative volume ~1.3). Standardisation rescales each one to (value − training mean) / training std dev.

It matters for logistic/linear regression with regularisation (otherwise the penalty treats features unfairly). Trees don't care: they only ask "above or below a threshold?".

Calculating the mean and std on the whole dataset leaks future information into training. Fit the scaler on training data only.

Overfitting

Memorising the exam answers instead of learning the subject. Great on questions you've seen, bad on new ones.

The model memorises quirks of the training data instead of general patterns. Signature: great training score, poor score on new dates.

With noisy financial data and ~1,750 training rows, overfitting is the default danger. That's why you compare a flexible model against a simple one and both against a baseline.

Link to the module: RNNs, transformers, LLMs

The fancy AI from the lectures (like the tech behind ChatGPT) needs mountains of data. You have about 2,700 days, so simple models are the right choice.

The machine translation lecture introduced RNNs, which read sequences in order, and LSTMs, which help them remember further back (fighting the vanishing gradient problem). Prices are sequences too, so an LSTM is a possible future extension.

But note the bidirectional RNN: it reads forwards and backwards. Brilliant for translation (you need the whole sentence), but in forecasting, reading backwards from the future is pure leakage.

Leakage vs overfitting

Overfitting is memorising. Leakage is cheating: accidentally letting the model see the answer. Leakage is worse because your score looks great but is fake.

Overfitting = learning noise. Leakage = using information you wouldn't have had at prediction time. Leakage is worse because it gives you a falsely great score.

Leakage examples in your project:

  • Today's Brent value that's published after the close.
  • Scaler fitted on all 2015–2025 data.
  • Rolling features that accidentally include tomorrow.
  • Peeking at test results, tweaking features, re-testing (repeat ~20 times and something will "work" by luck).

A scikit-learn Pipeline bundles scaler + model so the scaler is always fit only on whatever you're training on.

Baselines: the bar to beat

Mean absolute error (MAE)

On average, how far off were your guesses? If you guessed 1.2 and it was 1.5, you were 0.3 off. Average that over every day.

Unit 7

Black swans & fat tails

Why history-trained models break exactly when it matters most.

What is a black swan?

A shock almost nobody saw coming, that changes everything, and that people afterwards pretend was obvious.

Named by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (The Black Swan, 2007), after the European belief that all swans were white, until black swans were found in Australia. One observation broke a "law" built on thousands.

A black swan has three properties:

  1. Outlier: nothing in the past convincingly pointed to it.
  2. Extreme impact.
  3. Retrospective predictability: afterwards, everyone builds a story for why it was "obvious".

Related ideas: a grey swan is a known-but-unlikely risk. Taleb himself argued Covid was not a true black swan, because pandemics had been widely warned about. The market reaction, though, was still extreme.

The technical core: fat tails

Huge market crashes and spikes happen far more often than the textbook bell curve says they should.

Many models implicitly assume returns follow a normal (bell-curve) distribution. Real returns have fat tails: extreme days happen far more often than the bell curve allows. The statistic for this is excess kurtosis (normal = 0; daily stock returns are usually well above 0).

Normal vs fat-tailed (same volatility)

Why this matters: your validation period (2022–23) is an energy-crisis regime. A model chosen because it did well there may be tuned to crisis behaviour and look different on a calmer test period. That's regime change, one of your stated risks ("changing market relationships").

Why your models can't see them coming

A model learns from the past. If something has never happened before, there's nothing to learn from. It can only react once the chaos has started.

  • Supervised models learn from patterns in the training data. A truly novel event has no pattern to learn.
  • Your features are numerical market data. They react after news arrives; they don't contain the news. Your plan says this: "does not predict unexpected news".
  • A few extreme days can dominate a linear regression (squared errors punish big misses), pulling coefficients towards crisis behaviour.
  • MAE and accuracy averages can hide that a model was useless in exactly the 10 days that mattered.

What your models can do: once a shock starts, volatility clustering means recent-volatility features will raise the forecast quickly. They detect turbulence after it begins, not before.

How to handle this in your project

Don't delete the crazy days, look at them. Report how the model did in calm times and in chaotic times separately, and be honest that nobody can predict true surprises.

  1. Don't delete extreme days as "outliers". They're real, and they're the risk you care about. Investigate them in EDA instead.
  2. Report results by regime: e.g. calm vs turbulent sub-periods, or the 2022 shock separately, not just one overall average.
  3. Check kurtosis and plot the return histogram against a normal curve in EDA.
  4. Prefer MAE over squared-error metrics for reporting, as you planned: less dominated by a few huge errors.
  5. Optionally limit extreme feature values (winsorising), using thresholds learned from training data only.
  6. In limitations: say plainly that models cannot anticipate unprecedented events, and that the test period may not contain one.

3. Returns and the basket

Turn prices into daily percentage changes, then average Shell and BP to get your basket.

px = pd.DataFrame({"shel": shel["AdjClose"], "bp": bp["AdjClose"]})
rets = px.pct_change()                      # (today - yesterday) / yesterday

df = pd.DataFrame(index=px.index)
df["r"] = 0.5 * rets["shel"] + 0.5 * rets["bp"]   # daily-rebalanced 50/50
df["index"] = 100 * (1 + df["r"].fillna(0)).cumprod()  # basket index from 100

fillna(0) is fine here only for the first row (no previous day) when drawing the index; never for features.

4. Features (backward-looking only)

Build the clues using only past days: last return, last week's change, how bumpy recent weeks were, how busy trading was.

r = df["r"]
df["ret_1"]  = r                                   # known at today's close
df["ret_5"]  = (1 + r).rolling(5).apply(np.prod, raw=True) - 1
df["vol_5"]  = r.rolling(5).std()
df["vol_20"] = r.rolling(20).std()
for name, s in [("shel", shel), ("bp", bp)]:
    v = s["Volume"]
    df[f"relvol_{name}"] = v / v.rolling(20).mean()

.rolling(5) at row t uses rows t-4 … t. Backward only, so safe.

5. Targets (forward-looking)

Build the answers using future days: did it go up tomorrow, how bumpy were the next 5 days?

nxt = r.shift(-1)                                   # tomorrow's return, on today's row
df["y_dir"] = np.where(nxt.isna(), np.nan, (nxt > 0).astype(float))

# std of r[t+1..t+5]: rolling(5) ends at t+5, shift it back 5 rows
df["y_vol"] = r.rolling(5).std().shift(-5)

# date when each target is fully known (for boundary purging)
df["target_end"] = pd.Series(df.index, index=df.index).shift(-5)

Negative shift = look into the future. Only ever on y, never on X.

Bugs that silently ruin results

Small coding slip-ups that don't crash anything but secretly let the future leak in or make up fake data.

  • shift(-1) on a feature column → lookahead.
  • fillna(0) on returns → invents flat days.
  • train_test_split(shuffle=True) (the default!) → mixes past and future.
  • StandardScaler().fit(all_data) → leaks test statistics.
  • Forgetting random_state → results change each run.
  • Merging on date only → Brent from the future.
